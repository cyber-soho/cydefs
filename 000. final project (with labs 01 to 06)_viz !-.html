Where the contradictions live — check these four first

Address overlapLAB_01 zoning and LAB_05 firewall rules disagree about which subnet a zone actually uses.
Rule orderA permissive rule from LAB_01 sits above a deny rule from LAB_05, so the deny never fires.
Account driftThe service account created in LAB_02 is not the one the LAB_04 sensor or LAB_06 VPN authenticates with.
Protocol contradictionLAB_03 disabled a legacy protocol that a LAB_05 or LAB_06 configuration still depends on.

This is the part that earns the marks

Finding and resolving these collisions is the Final Project. A submission that lists three contradictions it found and explains how it fixed them scores higher than one that pretends there were none.

How your defence gets tested — the attacker's-eye view

The network you are defending is exactly the kind of network an intrusion tester is paid to break into. The smartest way to check your own work is to stop thinking like a builder for an hour and think like the attacker — every contradiction you leave in is a door they walk through.

⚔️ THE ATTACKER · red team

Prove your defence detects a real attack — not just that it is configured

This is in-scope defensive work — the competency requires rigorous validation of the effectiveness of your controls. On your own lab, against your own machines only:

1 · Attack
From a Kali VM you control, run an authorised nmap scan and one controlled test (e.g. an Atomic Red Team technique your IDS/IPS should catch) against a target you own.
2 · Capture
On the defender side, catch it: Wireshark on the LAN, plus your Suricata / Zeek / Wazuh alert if you deployed one.
3 · Write it up
What you fired, what the defence saw, and — honestly — what it missed. A control that blocked silently and logged nothing is half a control.

That write-up is worth more than any screenshot of a settings page, because it is evidence the defence works, not just evidence it exists. It is the strong version of section 5 in your project.

What to submit — two documents

Part A · 15%

The Final Project

One document, 10–16 pages, excluding evidence appendices.

  • 1Executive summaryOne page a non-technical partner could read: what the network protects, what it now resists, what remains a known risk.
  • 2Reference networkThe zone diagram, the address plan, the device inventory. One picture, one table.
  • 3Defence-in-depth mapEach of the seven layers, the control you built at it, and the lab it came from.
  • 4Integration findingsThe contradictions you found merging the six labs, and how you resolved each. Minimum three.
  • 5End-to-end verificationTests run on the assembled network — not the per-lab tests. Include at least one adversarial test (attack your own network, prove a control caught it).
  • 6Residual riskYour residual figure and your correlated failure modes. Hidden assumptions are not analysis.
  • 7RecommendationsWhat you would do next with more time or money, in priority order.
  • 8Evidence appendixScreenshots keyed to the evidence register. Every one carries an ID and a claim.
Part B · 10%

Course Procedure Manual

One chapter per lab, seven sections each — the skeleton rle01_cpm_scaffold.py made on day one.

  • 1ObjectiveNames the competency element it satisfies.
  • 2Scope and assumptionsStates what it covers and what it does not.
  • 3Pre-conditionsSnapshots, credentials, change window.
  • 4ProcedureNumbered. One action per step. No step needs you to explain it.
  • 5VerificationThe test that proves the control works, with the expected result.
  • 6RollbackHow to undo it when the change breaks production.
  • 7EvidenceFile names, command output, capture dates — cross-referenced.

The Manual test

Hand it to a classmate who was not in your group. If they can follow any chapter start to finish without asking you a single question, it is finished. If they ask even one, that answer belongs in the document.

Where you build all this — lab environment

Building on your own laptop (the common case)? Here is what you need. Everything runs offline in Oracle VirtualBox — nothing bridges to the real Internet, and your only targets are your own VMs.

ComponentMinimum (works)Recommended (comfortable)
CPU4 cores, VT-x / AMD-V enabled in BIOS/UEFI6–8 cores
RAM16 GB32 GB — biggest comfort factor
Free disk120 GB free (SSD)200 GB on NVMe SSD
Host OSWindows 10/11 64-bit, macOS 12+ (Intel), LinuxSame, fully updated

Software: VirtualBox 7.1.x + matching Extension Pack (free for education); an OPNsense or pfSense CE ISO for the edge firewall; a Windows evaluation VM and a Debian/Ubuntu Server VM as the hosts you defend; Wireshark; and a light Kali VM for the adversarial test above.

The #1 support ticket, every term

If VirtualBox only offers 32-bit guests or a VM won't start with a VT-x error, virtualisation is switched off in your firmware. Reboot into BIOS/UEFI and enable Intel VT-x or AMD-V / SVM. On Windows, also disable Hyper-V / “Memory Integrity” if it conflicts. Do this before the work session, not during it.

LAB_06 modernisation — use WireGuard

The current best practice for the VPN lab is WireGuard, now built into OPNsense 25.7 in-core (no plugin) — faster than OpenVPN with a smaller attack surface. If your LAB_06 used OpenVPN, say why WireGuard is preferred in your write-up.
