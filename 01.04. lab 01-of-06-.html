Words you'll meet today

Newbie IT glossary

You are new to Information Technology (IT). That is expected. Here are the only terms you need for this lab, in plain language.

Host
Any single computer on a network — a laptop, a server, a printer.
IP address
A computer's number on the network, e.g. 192.168.1.10. Like a street address for a house. (Internet Protocol address)
Port
A numbered “door” on a host for one kind of traffic (port 443 = secure web). A host has thousands.
Service
A program on a host that listens on a port and answers requests (a web server, a file server).
Firewall
A guard that decides which traffic may pass between networks, by rules you write.
Zone / VLAN
A group of hosts that trust each other, kept separate from others by a control point. (Virtual Local Area Network)
LAN / WAN
Your inside network / the outside world. (Local / Wide Area Network)
Loopback 127.0.0.1
A special address meaning “this machine only.” Traffic to it never touches a network card.
CLI
The text terminal where you type commands instead of clicking. (Command-Line Interface)
Script
A short text file of commands that runs them for you, the same way every time.
LLM
The kind of Artificial Intelligence (AI) behind chat assistants. It predicts text; it does not “know” things. (Large Language Model)
Ollama
A free program that runs an LLM entirely on your own computer — nothing leaves the machine.

Any other abbreviation in this page is spelled out in round brackets the first time it appears. Get in the habit of doing the same in your own writing — your marker will thank you.

A

Defense Methods with AI tools

≈ 75 min · Content Topic 1

Defense in depth means putting several independent controls on the same path, so that if one fails, the attacker is still not through. No single wall; many. A Security Operations Centre (SOC) — the team you are training to join — spends its day reading alerts and deciding which ones matter. That reading is slow and error-prone at 2 a.m., which is exactly where a local AI assistant earns its place: it drafts a plain-language summary, and a human verifies it. You will do both halves.

A1 · Local-AI alert triage with a privacy guard (≈ 45 min) — uses rle02

A real security alert is one of the most sensitive documents an organisation owns: it names hosts, accounts, internal addresses, and often the exact weakness that is currently unpatched. So it must never be pasted into a public chatbot. The script solves this two ways: it runs the model locally (on 127.0.0.1, which never touches a network card), and it redacts dangerous fields before the model sees anything.

Do this:

  1. Open the sample alert sample_alert.json and read it. Notice the notes field contains an e-mail address, a phone number, a pasted password, and a long code (a file hash). Three of those must never leave the building.
  2. Run the tool offline first, so you see the safe baseline:
    terminal
    python3 rle02_ai_triage_assistant.py --alert sample_alert.json --offline
    Open the decision_record.json it produced. Find the field literally named draft_not_verdict. Read that name — it is a control, not a decoration.
  3. Now run it with your local model and compare:
    terminal
    python3 rle02_ai_triage_assistant.py --alert sample_alert.json
  4. Open the record again and check the four analyst_* fields. They are empty on purpose — the record is structurally incomplete until a human types a verdict and a name. Fill them in: read the raw alert, decide whether it is real, and write your one-line verdict and your name.
  5. Prove the privacy guard works. In your own words, confirm that the password, e-mail, phone number, and hash from notes do not appear in the text that was sent to the model.
Deliverable A1(a) Your completed decision_record.json with your analyst verdict and name filled in; (b) two–three sentences comparing the offline draft with the model draft — which read better, and did the model assert anything the alert did not support? Finding that one over-reach is the actual skill.
🔑 Make it yours

Ollama is one choice. Any local model runner (for example Open WebUI — https://openwebui.com — as a front-end) is fine. If you swap it, write down what you used and why (see Section 8).

A2 · Measure the network, then reason about it (≈ 30 min) — uses rle05 + rle06

An assistant is only useful if you can measure what it is helping with.

  1. Open rle05_defence_readiness_console.html by double-clicking it (it works with the network unplugged — nothing is uploaded). Answer all fourteen questions honestly for your own home network. Read the final score, then export the CSV. The score is not the point; the rows that scored zero, in priority order, are.
  2. Run the resilience model to see why several ordinary controls beat one excellent one:
    terminal
    python3 rle06_resilience_model.py --scenario defended
    It prints the residual risk after each layer. Note the headline: seven ordinary layers leave far less residual risk than one strong control alone — because survival probabilities multiply, they do not add.
  3. Ask your local AI to help you explain the result in plain English for a non-technical client — then check its explanation against the numbers the script printed. If the model invents a figure, that is the teaching moment.
Deliverable A2(a) Your readiness CSV; (b) one short paragraph, in your own words, answering: “Why is a stack of several 45 %-effective controls a better investment than one 90 %-effective control?” — state the residual-risk figures, and name one assumption the model hides (hint: it assumes the layers fail independently, which is never fully true).
B

Zoning Concepts with AI tools

≈ 60 min · Content Topic 2

A zone is a group of hosts that share a trust level, separated from other zones by a control point that inspects the traffic between them. Two networks with a router and an “allow everything” rule are not two zones — they are one zone with extra paperwork. Before you can zone a network, you must first know every entry point: every door a host is offering to the world. You cannot defend a door you never counted.

B1 · Count the doors (≈ 25 min) — uses rle03

  1. Run the entry-point inventory on a machine you control (your own laptop, or the lab Linux virtual machine). It only reads — it changes nothing:
    terminal
    bash rle03_entry_point_inventory.sh --out entry_point_register.csv
  2. Open the CSV. Each row is a listening service, classified by exposure: ALL_INTERFACES (reachable from every network the host is on — including guest Wi-Fi), LOOPBACK_ONLY (this machine only — safest), or SPECIFIC_INTERFACE.
  3. See your own action become a door. In a second terminal, start a throw-away web server:
    terminal
    python3 -m http.server 8080
    Re-run the inventory. Watch a new ALL_INTERFACES row for port 8080 appear. Stop the server (Ctrl-C) and you have just opened and closed an entry point on purpose.
Deliverable B1Your entry_point_register.csv, plus one sentence per ALL_INTERFACES row answering: “Who needs to reach this service, and from which zone?” That single question is the whole of network defense, asked early.

B2 · Design the zones, let AI draft the rules, then verify (≈ 35 min)

Now you turn a flat network into a defensible one. Use this minimum zone plan for a small business as your starting point:

ZoneContainsRule of thumb
PerimeterThe firewall, the Virtual Private Network (VPN) gatewayNothing else lives here
ServersFile server, backup targetAccepts connections from Users; starts almost none itself
UsersStaff laptopsReaches Servers on named ports only
IoTPrinters, cameras, smart TVs (Internet of Things)Reaches the Internet if it must; nothing internal
GuestVisitor devicesInternet only — fully isolated from every other zone

Do this:

  1. Draw this as a simple diagram (paper, or any free tool such as draw.io — https://www.drawio.com). Label the control point between each zone.
  2. Use AI as a drafting assistant, not an oracle. Ask your local model to draft five plain-English firewall rules for the plan above — for example: “Users may reach Servers on port 445; Guest may reach only the Internet; deny everything not explicitly allowed and log it.” Let it write a first draft.
  3. Now verify every line yourself, because this is exactly where a model gets things subtly wrong. Check three things against the three rules of good zoning: (a) the last rule denies everything and logs it (default-deny); (b) each allow-rule names a flow, not a raw address, so it survives an address change; (c) no rule accidentally lets Guest reach an internal zone. Correct anything the model got wrong, and note what you corrected.
Deliverable B2(a) Your zone diagram; (b) your final five firewall rules after your corrections; (c) two–three sentences naming at least one thing the AI draft got wrong or unsafe, and how you fixed it. If the draft was perfect, say how you confirmed that — “it looked right” is not verification.
🔑 Make it yours

The five-zone plan, the tool, and the example rules are all a starting point. A different, well-justified zone design can score higher. Research how real small networks are segmented and improve on this.

The rules — read these, they matter

Open tools · creativity · lawful use
🤖 Everything here is open, and swapping is encouraged

Every tool, technique, address, and most of the content of these tasks is a starting point, not a commandment. If you find a better free tool, a cleaner method, or you want to build your own script, do it. The only condition: in your submission, write what you changed from the original and why. Research your tools thoroughly, compare alternatives, and be genuinely creative — that is the habit that makes an employable defender, and it earns marks rather than losing them.

⚠️ This is a training simulation, on systems you own

Some tools and techniques in this course resemble the real ones used by real attackers and defenders. That resemblance is the point of professional training — and it carries responsibility. Everything in this lab is to be run only on a computer you personally own, or on the lab environment assigned to you, never on the college network, an employer's systems, or anyone else's device without explicit, written authorisation. This applies to every technique in this course, including any not specifically named in this document. If you are ever unsure whether an action is allowed, it is not — stop, and ask your instructor in writing first.

⚠️ Lawful use only

Unauthorised access to, or interference with, a computer system is a criminal offence — in Canada under the Criminal Code, section 342.1 — https://laws-lois.justice.gc.ca/eng/acts/c-46/ — and under equivalent laws elsewhere (the United States Computer Fraud and Abuse Act, 18 U.S.C. §1030; European Union Directive 2013/40/EU; Spain's Ley Orgánica 10/1995). Personal information you encounter is protected under Quebec's Law 25 — https://www.cai.gouv.qc.ca/ — and Canada's PIPEDA (Personal Information Protection and Electronic Documents Act). “Hacking back” is never permitted, regardless of provocation.
